Signal grew out of a pattern seen repeatedly from the theatre, the intensive care unit and the ward: patients and families are asked to make consequential decisions around a hospital stay while being given the least explanation at exactly the moments it matters most. The middle stretch of that stay — the part patients are least able to picture, because they are frequently unconscious for it — is where an anaesthesiologist and intensivist spends their working life.

Why Signal exists

Signal explains the medical concepts people meet around a hospital stay — the symptoms that decide whether you need admission, the procedures that happen once you are inside, and the medicines you are sent home with.

Those three moments have something in common. They are where the stakes are highest, the time is shortest, and the explanation is thinnest. A person deciding at 2am whether chest pain warrants an emergency department, a person signing a consent form for a procedure they cannot picture, a person handed six boxes of tablets on discharge with no clear sense of which ones they must never stop — all three are being asked to act on information they were never really given.

That is the gap Signal is built for. Not a symptom checker, not a drug index, and not a replacement for the clinician in front of you — an explanation of the concepts, so that the conversation you have with your own doctor is a better one.

● The peri-hospitalisation arc

Organised by when you need it

Most health sites are organised by body system or by disease. Signal is organised by where you are in a hospital stay — because the question a person actually asks changes completely depending on the moment.

● Editorial policy

How entries are reviewed

Written by
Dr. Gopi Krishnan Palanivel, MD (Anaesthesia), MBA (Healthcare & Hospital Management) — specialist anaesthesiologist and intensivist at St. Isabel's Hospital, Mylapore, Chennai, and a practising geriatrician. Every entry on Signal is authored and edited by a named clinician, not assembled anonymously.
Independently reviewed
Not yet. Authorship and review are deliberately kept separate. An entry counts as reviewed only when a second named clinician has checked that specific entry — and until that happens, the entry says so on its face, in an amber notice you cannot miss.
No invented numbers
Frequency and risk are described in relative terms — common, uncommon, rare. We would rather say “uncommon” honestly than quote a precise-sounding figure that cannot be sourced.
No brands, no doses
The medicines section explains groups and classes of medicine and how they are used. It carries no brand names and no doses by design — it exists to correct dangerous misconceptions, never to instruct an individual.
Region-aware
Written with the Indian and South Asian context in mind, where that changes the picture — tuberculosis, dengue, earlier coronary disease, widespread iron deficiency, the over-the-counter steroid creams that turn a treatable fungal rash into a stubborn one.
Corrections
If something here is wrong, it should be fixed rather than defended. Report a correction.

What Signal can't do

It cannot examine you. It does not know your history, your medicines, your kidney function or what your own doctor has already told you — all of which change the answer.

So Signal never diagnoses, and never tells anyone to start or stop a treatment. Every symptom and every medicine names the signs that mean stop reading and seek care. Where an entry and your own clinician disagree, your clinician is right: they have the one thing this site will never have, which is you in front of them.

Where this stands today: Signal is an educational prototype. It is authored by a clinician, and it is not yet independently reviewed — and it tells you which of those is true on every single page.
